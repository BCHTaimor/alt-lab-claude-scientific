# Paper Analysis

Use this skill to explain what a paper actually established, rather than merely repeating its abstract or framing.

## Workflow

1. State the paper's main question and mechanistic claim.
2. Identify the experiments that carry most of the argument.
3. Separate direct observations from indirect inference.
4. Note limitations in the experimental system, assay, and interpretation.
5. Explain how later work strengthened, refined, or challenged the conclusions.

For older foundational papers, translate dated terminology and methods into current concepts without treating the paper as obsolete by default.

## Know What Source You Actually Have Before Analyzing

Before drafting an analysis, be clear about what's actually available: the full paper, just the text without figures, only the abstract, or someone else's summary of the paper. The depth and confidence of the analysis should match the source, not the topic's importance.

- With the full paper available, claims can be grounded in specific sections, figures, and page locations.
- With text only (no figures/tables), say so, and don't describe figure content as if it had been seen directly.
- With only an abstract or a secondhand summary, the analysis is necessarily shallow — flag this rather than filling in plausible-sounding experimental detail that wasn't actually in the source.

Never invent a page number, figure number, or specific experimental detail that isn't actually visible in the source being worked from. If the source is limited, the honest move is to say what's known and what isn't, not to extrapolate confidently.

## Build an Evidence Inventory Before Drafting the Analysis

For a full analysis of a paper (as opposed to a quick read), it helps to first inventory what the paper actually contains before writing the narrative:

- List the key figures, tables, or experiments and, for each, what specific claim it's meant to support.
- Note the experimental system, controls, and comparisons used for the central claims.
- Note limitations the authors themselves state, separately from limitations that seem apparent on critical reading but that the authors didn't raise.

Then check each central claim in the analysis against this inventory: does the evidence cited actually support the strength of the claim being made about it? A claim that outruns its supporting figure (e.g., a correlational result described as establishing causation) should be flagged as such rather than smoothed over.

## Keep the Paper's Claims and the Analysis's Interpretation Visibly Separate

When writing up the analysis, distinguish what the paper's authors concluded from any additional interpretation, connection to other work, or critique being added. A reader should be able to tell which parts are a description of what the paper showed and argued, and which parts are independent analytical commentary. This matters most for two things that are easy to blur:

- Ideas for follow-up experiments or extensions suggested during the analysis are hypotheses being proposed, not claims about what the paper itself established or a novelty claim on the paper's behalf.
- If external search is used to check how a specific result holds up against later literature, present that as an independent check, not as though it were part of the original paper's own discussion.

Before presenting the analysis, do a quick pass checking that: figure/data references match what's actually in the source, every substantive claim has something in the source (or an explicitly labeled outside check) backing it, and author conclusions are kept distinct from added interpretation.
