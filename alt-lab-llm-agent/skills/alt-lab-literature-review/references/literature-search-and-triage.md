# Literature Search and Triage

Use this skill to build a credible reading set before summarizing a field or recommending papers.

## Workflow

1. Define the mechanistic question, biological system, and intended use of the reading set.
2. Search for foundational studies, strong mechanistic work, relevant reviews, and recent developments.
3. Prefer primary research for central claims and use reviews to orient the search rather than replace it.
4. Group the result by purpose: foundational, recent, review, method, controversy, or directly actionable.
5. Explain why each selected paper is worth reading.

For broad questions, aim for enough diverse sources to cover each major subtopic rather than returning a short convenience list.

## Search Multiple Ways Before Concluding Coverage Is Thin

A single search phrasing or a single tool will systematically miss papers — different engines and different phrasings surface different subsets of the literature, and no one source is complete. Before treating a topic as thin or the search as done:

- Try more than one phrasing of the question (mechanistic terms, gene/protein names, assay names, and the more general biological process can each surface different papers for the same underlying question).
- Don't stop at the first tool or source that returns results. If a search interface or database seems to be missing obviously-relevant work, try another angle or another source rather than concluding the literature itself is sparse.
- Distinguish "review-only" coverage from real coverage: reviews are useful for orientation, but a reading set built only from what a couple of reviews cite will inherit those reviews' blind spots and framing. Go back to primary literature for the central claims.

## Weighing Papers When the Set Needs to Be Narrowed

When a search turns up more candidate papers than are useful to present, prioritize rather than truncating arbitrarily (e.g., by recency alone or by taking the first N results). Useful dimensions to weigh, roughly in order of importance for Alt Lab's mechanistic focus:

- How directly the paper addresses the mechanistic question at hand, versus being adjacent or background.
- The strength and directness of the experimental system relative to the claim (a paper making a claim with a well-controlled, direct experimental test outweighs one relying on indirect or purely correlative evidence).
- Whether the paper is foundational to the area (established the phenomenon or the central assay) versus incremental.
- How the paper connects to other work already in the reading set — does it fill a gap, corroborate a contested point, or introduce a genuinely new angle, versus duplicating coverage another selected paper already provides.

This is a way of thinking about trade-offs, not a formula to compute — the point is to be able to explain, for each included paper, what it specifically contributes that another paper in the set does not.

## Being Careful About Claims of Novelty or Priority

When a paper (or its introduction/discussion) claims to be first, novel, or unprecedented in some respect, or when synthesizing what a field has or hasn't already shown, do not treat the paper's own framing of its novelty as independently verified. A paper's related-work section reflects what its authors chose to cite, not a complete account of prior work. If a novelty or priority claim matters to the point being made, check it against the broader literature search rather than repeating the paper's self-characterization as settled fact.
